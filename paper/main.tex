\begin{abstract}
A retrieval-augmented system can only be as reliable as the collection it searches, yet the effect of
individual collection defects is seldom measured on its own. We inject four common defects into the
SciFact corpus, namely near-duplicate copies, stale copies with reversed findings, broken parsing and
fixed-size chunking, at rates of 10 to 50\% of the gold evidence. We then track, claim by claim, how
retrieval and the verdicts of four verifiers change. The verifiers are two zero-shot LLMs (Qwen2.5 1.5B
and 3B) and an NLI cross-encoder, used either off the shelf or fine-tuned. Every comparison is repeated
over five corruption seeds and three training seeds, and the main experiments are replicated on
Climate-FEVER. Copies push the gold document out of first place (dense recall@1 falls from 0.74 to 0.53
when half of the gold abstracts have a stale copy), but they hardly change the final verdicts. Broken
parsing behaves the other way round. Retrieval barely registers it, yet it costs correct answers for both
LLMs and for the fine-tuned verifier under every seed. Stale copies pull the fine-tuned verifier towards
\textsc{contradict}; on the one-sentence records of Climate-FEVER they cost it up to 55 correct answers
for 14 gained and make it more often confidently wrong, while both LLMs largely ignore them.
Single-sentence chunks make the fine-tuned verifier both less accurate and more confident. A team that
monitors retrieval quality alone would therefore rank these defects in the wrong order of harm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation \citep[RAG;][]{lewis2020rag} answers a question from documents it
retrieves out of a collection, so the answer can be no better than what the collection contains. Real
collections are rarely clean. The same document arrives from several sources, an outdated version sits
next to the revision that contradicts it, PDF extraction truncates or scrambles text, and chunking cuts
documents into pieces whose size is often chosen by habit. Most robustness studies of RAG intervene on
the reader's context, for example by inserting irrelevant or counterfactual passages
\citep{cuconasu2024noise,chen2024rgb,xie2024chameleon}. In practice, though, engineers do not edit the
context passage by passage. They maintain a collection, and they need to know which of its problems are
worth the cost of cleaning.

This paper asks how much each of four common defects, taken on its own, changes retrieval and the final
answer. We study claim verification because its datasets label both the verdict (does the evidence
support or contradict the claim?) and the evidence itself, which lets us follow a defect from the
retriever to the verdict. We inject one defect type at one rate at a time into an otherwise untouched
corpus and keep every model, prompt and threshold fixed, so that any change in the results can be
attributed to the corpus.

We make three contributions. First, we release a seeded corruption tool for four defect types that logs
every change it makes, together with a pipeline that regenerates each corrupted corpus from its seed and
checks it against the logged changes. Second, we report paired, claim-level measurements on SciFact
\citep{wadden2020scifact} for two retrievers, a reranker and four verifiers, repeated over five corruption
seeds and three verifier training seeds and replicated on Climate-FEVER \citep{diggelmann2020climatefever}.
Third, we show that retrieval metrics rank the defects in the wrong order of harm, that stale copies matter
mainly to verifiers that read each passage closely, and that very small chunks make a fine-tuned verifier
confidently wrong.

All code, per-claim predictions, corruption logs and fine-tuned models are publicly
available.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/Likhithvenkatanalla/rag-dq-benchmark}, archived at
\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23333457}.}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Robustness of retrieval-augmented models.}
Language models are easily distracted by irrelevant context \citep{shi2023distracted}, make poor use of
information in the middle of long inputs \citep{liu2024lost}, and can be trained to disregard irrelevant
passages \citep{yoran2024robust}. The RGB benchmark \citep{chen2024rgb} evaluates noise robustness,
negative rejection and counterfactual robustness of RAG readers, and \citet{cuconasu2024noise} report that
adding random documents to the context can even improve accuracy. These studies edit the reader's input
directly. We instead edit the collection and let the retriever decide what the reader sees, which is closer
to how such problems arise in deployed systems.

\paragraph{Knowledge conflicts.}
\citet{longpre2021conflicts} substitute entities in passages to create conflicts with a model's parametric
knowledge, and \citet{xie2024chameleon} find that LLMs readily accept conflicting evidence when it is
coherent. Our stale copies produce a different kind of conflict, one that lives inside the collection: the
original document and its reversed copy are both retrievable, much as an outdated and a current version of
a guideline can coexist in a document store.

\paragraph{Duplicates and data quality.}
Near-duplicates are pervasive in web-scale text, and removing them improves language-model training
\citep{lee2022dedup}. Retrieval benchmarks such as BEIR \citep{thakur2021beir} compare retrievers across
many clean collections. We take the opposite approach and hold the task fixed while degrading the
collection.

\paragraph{Claim verification.}
FEVER \citep{thorne2018fever}, SciFact \citep{wadden2020scifact} and Climate-FEVER
\citep{diggelmann2020climatefever} pair claims with evidence and a verdict. This makes the path from
evidence to answer measurable, which is why we use them here.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Data}
\paragraph{SciFact} contains expert-written scientific claims and a corpus of 5,183 abstracts. We evaluate
on the 300 development claims (124 \Sup, 64 \Con, 112 \Nei). Of these, 188 have gold evidence, drawn from
184 distinct abstracts. The 809 training claims, which do not overlap with the development set, are used
only for training and for tuning thresholds.

\paragraph{Climate-FEVER} pairs real-world climate claims with five annotated Wikipedia sentences each. We
build a closed corpus from all 5,240 distinct evidence sentences, which is close in size to SciFact; each
sentence becomes one record, titled with the name of its article. After removing the 154 claims labelled
\textsc{disputed}, 1,381 claims remain (654 \Sup, 253 \Con, 474 \Nei). The gold records of a claim are
its evidence sentences whose label matches the claim's verdict, and 907 claims have at least one. No model
in this study is trained on Climate-FEVER.

\subsection{Pipeline}
\paragraph{Retrieval.} We use BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25} over whitespace tokens and dense retrieval with
BGE-small-en-v1.5 \citep{xiao2023cpack} and exact cosine search \citep{johnson2019faiss}. Recall@$k$ is the
fraction of a claim's gold records found in the top $k$, averaged over claims that have evidence.

\paragraph{Verification.} Each verifier sees the claim together with the top three records from dense
retrieval and outputs \Sup, \Con\ or \Nei. \emph{Qwen-1.5B} and \emph{Qwen-3B} are Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct
and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25}, prompted zero-shot to answer with a single word and decoded
greedily. \emph{NLI zero-shot} is the cross-encoder \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-xsmall} \citep{he2023debertav3}
as released. \emph{NLI fine-tuned} is the same model after three epochs of training on 1,123 pairs built
from the SciFact training claims: each gold abstract with its label, plus \Nei\ examples taken from the
cited abstracts of claims without evidence and from the highest-ranked non-gold BM25 abstract of each claim
with evidence. We train it with three random seeds. The NLI verifiers score every (claim, record) pair
separately. A claim receives \Sup\ or \Con, whichever has the higher maximum probability across its three
records, unless neither reaches a threshold, in which case it receives \Nei. The threshold maximises
macro-F1 on 20\% of the training claims held out from training; the development set is never used for
tuning.

\paragraph{Reranking.} In one experiment a MiniLM cross-encoder \citep{nogueira2019rerank}, either as
released or fine-tuned on SciFact training data, reorders the dense top ten before verification.

\subsection{Defects}
Each defect is applied to 10, 25 or 50\% of the gold-evidence records, so that it actually reaches the
claims under evaluation. Claims and labels are never modified.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Duplicate} adds a lightly edited copy of a record. If the record has more than two sentences,
  one is dropped; about 5\% of the words are deleted, and one pair of adjacent words per sentence is
  swapped.
\item \textbf{Stale} adds a copy whose directional findings are reversed by rule
  (\emph{increased}$\leftrightarrow$\emph{decreased}, \emph{higher}$\leftrightarrow$\emph{lower},
  ``is associated'' $\rightarrow$ ``is not associated'', and so on). For Climate-FEVER the word list also
  includes climate terms such as \emph{warming}$\leftrightarrow$\emph{cooling} and
  \emph{rise}$\leftrightarrow$\emph{fall}. When a record contains none of these words, its copy is
  identical to the original, so we also analyse the reversed copies on their own.
\item \textbf{Parse} replaces a record with a broken version of it: truncated at 30 to 60\% of its length,
  with its sentences shuffled, or with OCR-style character errors.
\item \textbf{Chunk} splits every SciFact abstract into records of one, three or five sentences. It does not
  apply to Climate-FEVER, whose records are already single sentences.
\end{itemize}
A copy counts as a separate document, so retrieving it does not count as retrieving the gold record. A
chunk, by contrast, counts as the abstract it was cut from.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
All runs on a dataset are evaluated on the same claims, so we compare them in pairs. For every claim we
note whether the answer went from right to wrong (\emph{lost}) or from wrong to right (\emph{gained})
relative to the clean corpus, report both counts, and test them with an exact McNemar test
\citep{mcnemar1947}. Because we run many such tests without correcting for multiple comparisons, we
only treat an effect as a finding when its direction repeats across corruption seeds and, for the
fine-tuned verifier, across training seeds.

\section{Results on SciFact}

\subsection{Clean baseline}
Dense retrieval outperforms BM25 at every cut-off, with recall@1 of 0.744 against 0.615 and recall@10 of
0.957 against 0.883. Qwen-1.5B reaches 0.557 accuracy and 0.502 macro-F1, comfortably above the majority
class (0.413 and 0.195), but it answers \Sup\ for 200 of the 300 claims. The fine-tuned NLI verifier
reaches 0.650, 0.680 and 0.667 with its three training seeds, and each seed is significantly better than
Qwen-1.5B (lost/gained 32/60, 36/73 and 35/68; $p \le 0.005$). Without fine-tuning, the same NLI model
manages only 0.353. Doubling the size of the LLM closes the gap. Qwen-3B reaches 0.667 accuracy and
0.655 macro-F1, significantly better than Qwen-1.5B (34 lost, 67 gained; $p=0.001$) and on par with the
fine-tuned verifier, without any task-specific training. Its answers are also far more balanced
(109 \Sup, 96 \Con, 95 \Nei).

Copies push the gold abstract out of first place, and the more copies there are, the larger the drop
(Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}, Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}). Recall@10 does not move at all, because a copy
typically lands just above its original. Averaged over five corruption seeds, dense recall@1 at the 50\%

duplicates, but very little of the loss caused by stale copies. This is to be expected, since a reversed
copy is just as relevant to the claim as its original and a relevance model has no reason to prefer one
over the other. Broken parsing puts some documents out of BM25's reach (recall@10 drops to 0.845 at 50\%)
while leaving dense retrieval almost untouched. Chunking hurts BM25 at every size and has no clear effect
on dense retrieval.

For most defects the change in accuracy is small (Table~\ref{tab:verification},
Figure~\ref{fig:verification}), so we look at the paired, claim-level counts instead.

\paragraph{Broken parsing costs correct answers.} Across five corruption seeds, the fine-tuned verifier
(training seed 0) lost more correct answers than it gained in 14 of the 15 parse runs, and in no run did it
gain more than it lost (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). Summed over seeds, it lost 41 answers and gained 13 at the
50\% level, and lost 18 and gained 4 at 25\%. None of these runs is significant on its own at $p<0.05$
(the strongest has 12 lost against 4 gained), which is exactly why the repetition matters: if parsing had no
effect, a net loss on all five seeds would occur with probability $1/32$ at each rate. The other verifiers
point the same way. Qwen-1.5B lost 8 answers and gained none at 50\% ($p=0.008$), all three training seeds
of the fine-tuned verifier lost more than they gained on corruption seed 0, and Qwen-3B lost more than it
gained at both 25\% (5 against 2) and 50\% (13 against 7).

\paragraph{Single-sentence chunks hurt the fine-tuned verifier.} The effect holds for every training seed
(mean accuracy 0.573; lost/gained 58/38, 65/32 and 64/34, with $p=0.052$, $0.001$ and $0.003$). The model
was trained on whole abstracts, and five-sentence chunks do it no harm. Neither Qwen model is hurt by
chunking.

\paragraph{Duplicates leave verification unchanged.} Although duplicates have the clearest effect on
retrieval, the gold abstract still reaches the top three for almost as many claims as before (87.2\% on the
clean corpus and 86.7\% at 50\%), and a near-copy carries the same evidence anyway. Over five seeds the
fine-tuned verifier even gained slightly more answers than it lost at 25 and 50\% (12 lost, 28 gained in
total).

\paragraph{Reranking does little for verification.} Verifying from the reranked top three changes accuracy
by between 0 and +2.3 points across the 13 corpora, and no change is significant. The gold abstract is
usually already among the dense top three, and its exact position there matters little to the verifier.

\subsection{Conflicting evidence}

To study genuine conflicts, we restrict the analysis to claims whose top three records included a stale copy
in which at least one finding was actually reversed, and compare each of these claims with its outcome on
the clean corpus. At the 50\% level on corruption seed 0, 78 claims meet this condition. The fine-tuned
verifier changed its answer on 13, 19 and 16 of them (training seeds 0, 1 and 2), and 11, 15 and 12 of those
changes were to \Con. Qwen-1.5B changed only 6 answers and moved 1 to \Con, which fits its general
preference for \Sup. One might suspect that this indifference is simply a symptom of that bias, but
Qwen-3B, which is as accurate as the fine-tuned verifier and much more balanced, behaves the same way: it
changed 8 of the 78 answers and moved 3 to \Con\ (4 lost, 4 gained).

The behaviour of the fine-tuned verifier is stable across corruption seeds. Of the 48 answers it changed at
the 50\% level over five seeds, 35 moved to \Con, and claims whose context contained only unreversed copies
never changed. Even so, no verifier's accuracy changes significantly on SciFact. The fine-tuned verifier is
misled on some claims and corrected on others (23 lost and 22 gained over five seeds), and the reversed
copies do not make it confidently wrong more often: on these claims it gave 55 such answers on the clean
corpus and 56 with stale copies. The count rose on two seeds and fell on three, so an increase that appeared
on a single seed in our earlier analysis does not hold up.

\subsection{Calibration}

We assign each NLI answer a confidence score. For \Sup\ or \Con\ this is the highest probability of that
label among the three records, and for \Nei\ it is one minus the highest \Sup\ or \Con\ probability. We then
compare confidence with correctness. The Qwen models produce no probabilities and are left out of this
analysis. Even on clean data the fine-tuned verifier is overconfident: its mean confidence is 0.75--0.80
while its accuracy is 0.65--0.68, its expected calibration error \citep[ECE;][]{guo2017calibration} is
0.14--0.18, and 8--11\% of claims are answered wrongly with a confidence of at least 0.9, which we call
\emph{confidently wrong}. Copies and broken parses hardly change these figures, but small chunks do. With
one-sentence records the verifier becomes more confident while its accuracy drops, its ECE roughly doubles
to 0.31--0.32 for all three training seeds, and 18--22\% of claims are confidently wrong
(Figure~\ref{fig:confwrong}).

Climate-FEVER is a demanding test for our findings. Its records are single Wikipedia sentences, its claims
were collected from the web rather than written by experts, and none of our verifiers was trained on it.
Retrieval is much harder than on SciFact: a gold sentence reaches the dense top three for only 53\% of
claims with evidence, compared with 87\% on SciFact. Both verifiers are weak as well. Qwen-1.5B reaches
0.508 accuracy against a majority baseline of 0.474 and predicts \Nei\ for only 89 claims, and the
SciFact-trained NLI verifier transfers poorly, reaching 0.458 on average (0.450--0.468) and falling
significantly below Qwen-1.5B for every training seed ($p \le 0.039$). We therefore focus on the effects
that do not depend on a verifier being accurate in absolute terms (Table~\ref{tab:climate}).

\paragraph{Stale copies hurt the NLI verifier far more than on SciFact.} At the 25 and 50\% levels every
training seed lost many more correct answers than it gained. At 50\% the counts were 55/14, 46/16 and 48/15,
all with $p<0.001$, and accuracy declined steadily with the corruption rate for each seed. Among the 356
claims with a reversed copy in their top three at 50\%, the verifier changed between 84 and 101 answers,
roughly half of them to \Con, and became confidently wrong more often under all three seeds (98 to 119, 95
to 120 and 79 to 107). Qwen-1.5B once again hardly reacted (26 lost, 27 gained). We think the difference from
SciFact comes from the length of the records. A one-sentence record offers nothing else that could outweigh a
reversed finding, whereas a full abstract usually does.

\paragraph{Copies displace gold records without hurting verification.} Duplicates and stale copies both
reduce the gold-in-top-3 rate by about four points at 50\%. Duplicates nonetheless leave accuracy
essentially unchanged; the fine-tuned verifier moves by at most 0.4 points, and Qwen-1.5B even improves at
25\% (4 lost, 21 gained).

\paragraph{Broken parsing shows no consistent effect.} With one-sentence records, a third of the parse
corruptions (shuffling the sentence order) change nothing at all, and truncation still leaves part of a short
sentence intact. The lost and gained counts point in both directions across verifiers and seeds.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Retrieval metrics can rank defects in the wrong order.} On SciFact, duplicates and stale copies
cause the largest drops in recall@1, yet they do not reliably change verification, because the verifier reads
the top three records and the gold abstract seldom drops out of them. Broken parsing is the opposite case.
Retrieval barely notices it, but it is the defect that most consistently costs correct answers, for both LLMs
and for the fine-tuned verifier under all of its training and corruption seeds, because the verifier is handed
a truncated or garbled version of the right abstract. A team that tracked retrieval metrics alone would
prioritise these defects the wrong way round. Evaluating final answers on a deliberately corrupted copy of
one's own collection is inexpensive and avoids this mistake.

\paragraph{Whether conflicting evidence matters depends on the reader.} The fine-tuned NLI verifier judges each
record on its own and responds to reversed copies. On SciFact it shifts answers towards \Con\ without a net
loss in accuracy, and on Climate-FEVER, where each record is a single sentence, it loses many correct answers
and becomes confidently wrong more often. Both zero-shot LLMs mostly ignore the reversed copies, and this
includes Qwen-3B, which is as accurate as the fine-tuned verifier on clean SciFact. Reranking by relevance
cannot fix the problem, since a reversed copy is exactly as relevant as its original. Detecting conflicting
versions requires other signals, such as dates, provenance, or deduplication before documents are indexed.

\paragraph{Chunk size should match the verifier's training.} The fine-tuned verifier, which was trained on whole
abstracts, performs worse on single sentences and grows more confident as it does so. Chunking choices should
therefore follow the input format a verifier was trained on, and its confidence scores should not be trusted
outside that format.

\section*{Limitations}

On SciFact an effect involving only a few of the 300 claims is within noise, and our McNemar $p$-values are not
corrected for multiple comparisons. Our conclusions therefore rest on effects that repeat across corruption
seeds, training seeds, verifiers and datasets. The Qwen-3B and Climate-FEVER experiments use a single corruption
seed. Stale copies are generated by rule-based word substitution and may read less naturally than genuinely
outdated documents. Some copies contain nothing to reverse (about 20\% on SciFact and 32\% on Climate-FEVER),
which is why we analyse the reversed copies separately. Because the defects target gold-evidence records, their
effect on the evaluated claims is easy to see, but this does not reflect how defects are distributed in real
collections. Finally, all models are small (BGE-small, Qwen2.5 up to 3B, DeBERTa-v3-xsmall), since every
experiment ran on free CPU hardware. Larger readers may react differently, and we did not study open-ended
generation beyond a one-word verdict.

\section*{Ethics and Reproducibility Statement}
SciFact and Climate-FEVER are public research datasets. We download them from their official releases and do
not redistribute them. All experiments ran on free GitHub Actions CPU runners, and the workflows that produced
every reported number are included in the repository. Each corrupted corpus is regenerated from its seed and
checked against the committed log of changes before use. An AI assistant (Claude) was used to help write code
and to draft and edit text; the author reviewed all of it, and every reported number comes from runs whose
